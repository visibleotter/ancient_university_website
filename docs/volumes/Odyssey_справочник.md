## Notes on Wilson's Translation

*Translation choices, register, and any notable departures from the Greek worth flagging — Wilson's translation is known for its plain, contemporary diction (deliberately avoiding archaism, unlike Gnedich's approach to the Iliad) and for certain interpretive choices (e.g. "complicated" for πολύτροπος in line 1) that have drawn scholarly discussion.*

**πολύτλας δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς — "much-enduring, godlike Odysseus."** One of Odysseus's most frequent recurring epithets. **πολύτλας** (polýtlas) — "having borne much" (πολύ- "much" + τλάω "to endure, dare") — attested in Homer only in the nominative, exclusively as an epithet of Odysseus (also found in Sophocles). LSJ glosses it as "having borne much, much-enduring," with a secondary shade of "one who dares much." **δῖος** — "divine, godlike, shining," same root as Ζεύς/Διός; A. T. Murray's classic 1919 translation renders the formula as "much-enduring, goodly Odysseus," likely softening "godlike" deliberately.

Wilson notably chose not to preserve this (or most) formulaic epithets identically at every recurrence, unlike most translators historically. This is only one epithet among a whole cluster attached to Odysseus, mostly built on the prefix πολυ- ("much/many"): πολυμήχανος ("of many devices"), πολύμητις ("of many wiles/much cunning"), πολύφρων ("much-thinking"), as well as πτολίπορθος ("sacker of cities"), διογενής ("Zeus-born"), Λαερτιάδης ("son of Laertes") — Homer characterizes Odysseus through sheer multiplicity of attributes, intensely possessed.

This directly connects to the poem's opening word, **πολύτροπον** (accusative of πολύτροπος, "much-turned/turning"), which Wilson rendered as "complicated" — a choice that generated substantial discussion (see her essay "On Complicated"). Wilson notes that Homer had a whole set of standing epithets to choose from for Odysseus's first appearance — intelligence, cunning, endurance, closeness to the gods — and the choice of the comparatively unusual πολύτροπος for the poem's first line is likely programmatic, inviting the audience to think about the hero's journey and the poem itself as full of turns.

Beyond the core "much-enduring" formula, rarer synonyms scattered through the poem extend the same semantic field: πολυπενθής ("much-grieving," 14.386), πολυτλήμων ("truly much-enduring," 18.319), πολύστονος ("much-suffering," 19.118) — the theme of Odysseus's suffering is carried not by one fixed formula but by a whole cloud of closely related words.

## Line-by-Line Analysis (by book, as read)

### Book 1

**Lines 187–193 — Athena disguised as Mentes, on Laertes.** Wilson: "I hear that fine old man no longer comes to town, but lives out in the countryside, stricken by grief, with only one old slave, who gives him food and drink when he trails back leg-weary from his orchard, rich in vines." Greek: **ξεῖνοι δ᾽ ἀλλήλων πατρώιοι εὐχόμεθ᾽ εἶναι ἐξ ἀρχῆς, εἴ πέρ τε γέροντ᾽ εἴρηαι ἐπελθὼν Λαέρτην ἥρωα, τὸν οὐκέτι φασὶ πόλινδε ἔρχεσθ᾽, ἀλλ᾽ ἀπάνευθεν ἐπ᾽ ἀγροῦ πήματα πάσχειν γρηὶ σὺν ἀμφιπόλῳ, ἥ οἱ βρῶσίν τε πόσιν τε παρτιθεῖ, εὖτ᾽ ἄν μιν κάματος κατὰ γυῖα λάβῃσιν ἑρπύζοντ᾽ ἀνὰ γουνὸν ἀλωῆς οἰνοπέδοιο** — "we claim to be hereditary guest-friends (ξεῖνοι πατρώιοι) of one another from of old; if you should go and ask the old man, the hero Laertes, they say he no longer comes to town, but suffers hardships far off on his farm with an old slave-woman, who sets out food and drink for him when weariness overtakes his limbs as he crawls up the slope of his vineyard."

Key vocabulary: **ξεῖνοι πατρώιοι** — "hereditary guest-friends," the same concept of **ξενία** already analyzed in the Iliad document (Glaucus and Diomedes, Book VI) — Athena-as-Mentes builds her cover story on inherited guest-friendship, which both makes the disguise plausible and thematically anticipates the poem's central concern with hospitality and its abuse (the suitors). **πήματα** — "sufferings, woes" — same word πῆμα analyzed in the Iliad for Paris ("nurtured as a doom," Il. 6.281) — here signals genuine grief. **ἑρπύζοντ(α)** — "crawling, creeping" (ἕρπω), a strikingly undignified verb applied to a man just called ἥρωα ("hero") — sharp contrast between epic title and the physical indignity of old age. **ἀλωῆς οἰνοπέδοιο** — "vineyard" — a detail that pays off structurally in Book 24, at the final recognition scene between Odysseus and Laertes in this same vineyard.

*Scholarly note.* Per the Dickinson College Commentaries note on this passage, Laertes's self-imposed rural isolation works on several levels at once: (1) it is psychologically realistic — an old man grieving his lost son who avoids public life; (2) it plants a narrative detail that only pays off 23 books later, at the Book 24 recognition scene in this same vineyard; (3) it reflects the poem's broader theme of household (οἶκος) collapse in the absence of its rightful head — not only is Penelope besieged by suitors, but the older generation (Laertes) has been displaced from normal social life, physically removed from the polis.

*Connection to the Iliad document:* worth flagging as an instance of the **disguise-scene** as a recurring type-scene — a god appearing under a constructed false identity with its own coherent backstory. This becomes a structural mainstay of the Odyssey (Odysseus himself operates under disguise for much of the poem's second half), distinct from the more occasional, often unseen divine interventions typical of the Iliad.

**Lines 214–220 — Telemachus doubts his own paternity.** Wilson: "since no one knows his own begetting. I wish I were the son of someone lucky, who could grow old at home with all his wealth." Greek: **τοιγὰρ ἐγώ τοι, ξεῖνε, μάλ᾽ ἀτρεκέως ἀγορεύσω. μήτηρ μέν τέ μέ φησι τοῦ ἔμμεναι, αὐτὰρ ἐγώ γε οὐκ οἶδ᾽: οὐ γάρ πώ τις ἑὸν γόνον αὐτὸς ἀνέγνω. ὡς δὴ ἐγώ γ᾽ ὄφελον μάκαρός νύ τευ ἔμμεναι υἱὸς ἀνέρος, ὃν κτεάτεσσιν ἑοῖς ἔπι γῆρας ἔτετμε** — "So I will tell you very plainly, stranger. My mother says I am his son, but I myself do not know — for no one ever yet knew his own begetting for certain. How I wish, instead, I were the son of some fortunate man whom old age overtook among his own possessions."

Key vocabulary: **αὐτὸς ἀνέγνω** ("knew/recognized for himself," from ἀναγιγνώσκω) — Telemachus universalizes his personal uncertainty into an epistemological claim that *no one* can verify their own paternity; **ἀτρεκέως** ("exactly, precisely") — ironic, since what follows is an admission of not knowing something exactly; **κτεάτεσσιν... γῆρας ἔτετμε** ("old age overtook him among his possessions") — the fantasy is not about a *better* father emotionally, but about undisturbed inheritance and property — a concrete, economic wish.

*Scholarly commentary.* Jenny Strauss Clay notes the deep irony that Odysseus is actually present in the room, disguised and unrecognized, while Telemachus voices doubt about his own identity — his avoidance of even naming his father throughout the Book 1 dialogue with Athena/Mentes is a recurring pattern, and this is its most striking expression. The *Classical Quarterly* article "The Fathers of Telemachus" connects this to his stated grievance a few lines later (231–244): he would not be so grieved had Odysseus died gloriously at Troy with a burial mound and kleos to pass on — instead the storm winds swept him away anonymously, leaving Telemachus only pain. His complaint is less about *whether* Odysseus is his father than about the absence of a legible, honorable kleos-narrative to inherit.

*Connection to the Iliad document:* a striking inversion of Iliadic norms, where lineage and patronymic identity anchor heroic self-presentation (cf. Glaucus's genealogical speech to Diomedes, Il. VI, and the constant patronymics — Πηλεΐδης, Ἀτρεΐδης). Here, in the Odyssey's opening book, the hero's own son publicly doubts his patronymic legitimacy — a departure from the Iliadic identity-through-lineage model that sets up one of the Odyssey's central arcs: Telemachus's coming-of-age and eventual claim to his father's name and house.

**Lines 236–243 — Telemachus on kléos denied.** Wilson: "But now, the winds have seized him, and he is nameless and unknown. He left nothing but tears for me." Greek: **οὔ κε θανόντι περ ὧδ᾽ ἀκαχοίμην, εἰ μετὰ οἷσ᾽ ἑτάροισι δάμη Τρώων ἐνὶ δήμῳ, ἠὲ φίλων ἐν χερσίν, ἐπεὶ πόλεμον τολύπευσε. τῶ κέν οἱ τύμβον μὲν ἐποίησαν Παναχαιοί, ἠδέ κε καὶ ᾧ παιδὶ μέγα κλέος ἤρατ᾽ ὀπίσσω. νῦν δέ μιν ἀκλειῶς Ἅρπυιαι ἀνηρέψαντο· οἴχετ᾽ ἄϊστος ἄπυστος, ἐμοὶ δ᾽ ὀδύνας τε γόους τε κάλλιπεν** — "I would not grieve so much for him, even dead, if he had fallen among his companions in the land of the Trojans, or in his friends' arms, once he had wound up the thread of war. Then the Panachaeans would have made him a burial mound, and he would have won great kléos for his son too, hereafter. But now, ingloriously, the Harpies have snatched him away; he is gone, unseen, unheard-of, and he left me only pains and laments."

Key vocabulary: **ἀκλειῶς** ("without kléos") — the negative adverb of κλέος itself, the structural pivot of the whole speech; **Ἅρπυιαι ἀνηρέψαντο** ("the Harpies snatched him") — a mythologized image for ordinary, untraceable death at sea; **ἄϊστος ἄπυστος** — tight alliterative doublet, "unseen" (ἀ- + εἶδον) / "unheard-of" (ἀ- + πεύθομαι) — total erasure on both the visual and narrative-transmission axes; **τύμβον... μέγα κλέος** — tomb and glory presented as a single package: a marked grave is the precondition for kléos to be transmitted to the son.

*Scholarly framing.* This is one of the poem's clearest programmatic statements of the Odyssey's anxiety about **kléos without a body** — an anti-monument. Compare directly to the Iliad's logic already logged in the Iliad document (VI.55–60): there, ἀκήδεστοι/ἄφαντοι (unburied, erased) is the *threatened punishment* Agamemnon wishes on the Trojans, the worst imaginable fate. Here, that same fate — unmarked, untold disappearance — has already happened to the hero's own father, in his own son's eyes. Telemachus describes Odysseus as having suffered the Iliadic curse-fate without deserving it, purely by vanishing at sea instead of dying gloriously in battle. Worth flagging as a genre-level contrast: the Iliad's economy of glory runs through witnessed, memorialized death in battle; the Odyssey opens by dramatizing what happens to a hero who falls outside that economy — a son left with only γόοι (laments) instead of κλέος (glory).

**Lines 319–323 — Athena departs as a bird; Telemachus realizes.** Wilson: "Watching her go, he was amazed and saw she was a god." Greek: **ἡ μὲν ἄρ᾽ ὣς εἰποῦσ᾽ ἀπέβη γλαυκῶπις Ἀθήνη, ὄρνις δ᾽ ὣς ἀνόπαια διέπτατο: τῷ δ᾽ ἐνὶ θυμῷ θῆκε μένος καὶ θάρσος, ὑπέμνησέν τέ ἑ πατρὸς μᾶλλον ἔτ᾽ ἢ τὸ πάροιθεν. ὁ δὲ φρεσὶν ᾗσι νοήσας θάμβησεν κατὰ θυμόν: ὀίσατο γὰρ θεὸν εἶναι** — "So having spoken, grey-eyed Athena departed, and flew off like a bird, straight up; and she put strength and courage into his heart, and reminded him of his father even more than before. And perceiving this in his mind, he was amazed in his heart — for he supposed she was a god."

Key vocabulary: **ὄρνις... διέπτατο** — "flew off like a bird" (διαπέτομαι) — a literal transformation, not merely a simile, consistent with Homeric divine disguise/exit conventions; **ἀνόπαια** — an extremely rare, obscure word (a Homeric hapax legomenon, occurring nowhere else in the corpus) — already the ancient Alexandrian scholars (including, per the biographical tradition, Aristophanes of Byzantium) disputed its meaning: some read it as "straight up, vertically," others connected it to a supposed bird-name (a kind of sea-eagle) that Athena turned into; **μένος καὶ θάρσος** ("strength and courage") — the identical formula Athena uses on Diomedes before his aristeia in the Iliad (Il. V.2) — a direct cross-poem textual echo; **ὀίσατο γὰρ θεὸν εἶναι** ("for he supposed she was a god") — notably, Telemachus does not recognize Athena by appearance; the realization comes only retroactively, from the fact of her sudden flight, not from unmasking a disguise.

*Function of the scene:* a marker of Telemachus's coming-of-age — the god here literally instills courage and resolve (θῆκε μένος καὶ θάρσος) through the act of departure itself, not only through prior speech. Immediately afterward Telemachus acts with new decisiveness, approaching the suitors "like a godlike man" (ἰσόθεος φώς, line 324) — a marked shift in self-presentation right after the epiphany.

*Connection to the Iliad document:* the reused formula μένος καὶ θάρσος (cf. Il. V.2, Athena arming Diomedes for his aristeia) is worth flagging in the "Connections" section as a shared formulaic building-block Athena specifically uses to energize a young/untested hero at a critical narrative threshold in both poems.

**Lines 346–349 — Telemachus on the singer and Zeus's responsibility.** Wilson: "Poets are not to blame for how things are; Zeus must be the one who is to blame." Greek: **μῆτερ ἐμή, τί τ᾽ ἄρα φθονέεις ἐρίηρον ἀοιδὸν τέρπειν ὅππῃ οἱ νόος ὄρνυται; οὔ νύ τ᾽ ἀοιδοὶ αἴτιοι, ἀλλά ποθι Ζεὺς αἴτιος, ὅς τε δίδωσιν ἀνδράσιν ἀλφηστῇσιν, ὅπως ἐθέλῃσιν, ἑκάστῳ** — "My mother, why do you begrudge the faithful singer his pleasure, delighting us wherever his mind is stirred? It is not the singers who are to blame, but Zeus somehow is to blame, who gives to bread-eating men, to each, as he wishes."

Key vocabulary: **αἴτιοι/αἴτιος** ("responsible, to blame") — a juridically-coded word, root of the later philosophical term αἰτία ("cause," central to Aristotle); **ἐρίηρον ἀοιδόν** — "the faithful/trusty singer" (ἐρι- intensive prefix + ἠρός "trusty"), epithet of the bard Phemius; **ἀνδράσιν ἀλφηστῇσιν** — "bread-eating men" (ἀλφηστής, from ἄλφι "barley meal") — a rare, archaic epithet for mortals, marking their earthly, bodily nature as opposed to the gods.

*Scholarly framing, three layers:* (1) **Theological** — Telemachus articulates what scholars call the **Διὸς βουλή** ("plan/will of Zeus") theme, announced already in the Iliad's proem (Διὸς δ᾽ ἐτελείετο βουλή, Il. I.5) and structuring both poems: Zeus as ultimate cause of human fortunes. CHS scholarship notes this is the first time in the poem Telemachus articulates this theodicy explicitly in his own voice. (2) **Metapoetic** — one of the few Homeric statements on the nature of poetry itself: the singer bears no responsibility for a song's content (here, the grievous song of the Achaeans' homecoming, which pains Penelope), since inspiration comes from a god rather than personal choice — arguably the earliest known defense of a performer's artistic freedom from responsibility for content. (3) **Status/compositional** — per the Wikipedia article on the "Telemachy" (drawing on scholarship on the poem's structure), this is the first moment in the poem where Telemachus asserts himself as head of household: he follows this remark by directly ordering Penelope back to her weaving and loom ("speech/decision-making is men's business, mine above all, since I am master in this house") — the discussion of poetics becomes the occasion for his first act of self-assertion as household authority.

*Connection to the Iliad document:* directly parallels the Mοῖρα–Zeus tension line already logged for Il. XVI (Sarpedon) — there, ultimate divine responsibility governs mortal battlefield fates; here, the identical principle (gods, not humans, bear final responsibility) is applied to the far humbler but telling domain of a performed song's content.

**Lines 413–416 — Telemachus rejects rumor and prophecy.** Wilson: "My father is not coming home. I do not listen to any gossip, or forecasts from the psychics whom my mother invites to visit us." Greek: **Εὐρύμαχ᾽, ἦ τοι νόστος ἀπώλετο πατρὸς ἐμοῖο: οὔτ᾽ οὖν ἀγγελίῃ ἔτι πείθομαι, εἴ ποθεν ἔλθοι, οὔτε θεοπροπίης ἐμπάζομαι, ἥν τινα μήτηρ ἐς μέγαρον καλέσασα θεοπρόπον ἐξερέηται** — "Eurymachus, my father's homecoming is truly lost/destroyed; I no longer trust any report, wherever it might come from, nor do I heed any prophecy, whichever one my mother, calling a seer into the hall, seeks out."

Key vocabulary: **νόστος ἀπώλετο** ("the homecoming has perished," ἀπόλλυμι "to destroy") — a strong, near-final formulation: not "my father is dead" but the very *possibility of return* is declared lost, narrower and more bitter grammatically; **ἀγγελίῃ πείθομαι** — "I trust a report" (ἀγγελία "message," root of later "angel"); **θεοπροπίης ἐμπάζομαι** — "I heed a prophecy" (θεοπροπίη "divine utterance," from θεός + a root meaning "to declare"); **θεοπρόπον** — "seer, diviner" (literally "one who declares from a god") — a specific institutional figure of Homeric society, formally invited into the house, not a vague folk term.

*Scholarly/contextual note.* This is Telemachus's direct reply to the suitor Eurymachus's mocking question about the mysterious guest (Athena-as-Mentes, who has just departed). It shows him in a state of hardened, adult skepticism: he explicitly rejects both informal rumor and formal, institutionally-sanctioned prophecy as sources of hope — not from indifference, but from having survived too many false consolations already. This sharply contrasts with Penelope, who (as the poem shows elsewhere) does consult such θεοπρόποι and continues to hope. The epistemic gap between mother and son is a real narrative engine: Telemachus's coming journey to Nestor and Menelaus (Books 3–4) is motivated precisely by his refusal to rely on unverified report at home — he needs independently secured, reliable knowledge instead.

Notable irony: immediately after this speech (line 420), Telemachus finally recognizes that his guest was an immortal god (ὣς φάτο Τηλέμαχος, φρεσὶ δ᾽ ἀθανάτην θεὸν ἔγνω) — his demonstrative skepticism toward seers and rumor sits right next to the fact that genuine revelation had just come to him not through a θεοπρόπος, but from the goddess herself, in person, unrecognized until the moment of her departure.

## Connections to the Iliad

*Shared formulas, themes, and scholarly frameworks — cross-referenced to the Iliad document.*

- **ξενία (guest-friendship)** — Od. 1.187–193 (Athena-as-Mentes constructs her disguise around inherited guest-friendship) directly parallels Il. VI.119–236 (Glaucus and Diomedes) — same social institution as a structuring device.
- **kléos and the unburied/unremembered dead** — Od. 1.236–243 (Telemachus on Odysseus's ἀκλειῶς, unmarked disappearance) inverts the logic of Il. VI.55–60 (Agamemnon's wish that the Trojans die ἀκήδεστοι καὶ ἄφαντοι). In the Iliad this fate is a threatened curse on an enemy; in the Odyssey it has actually befallen the poem's own hero, as reported by his grieving son — a genre-level shift from glory won through witnessed battle-death to anxiety about glory denied through anonymous disappearance.
- **μένος καὶ θάρσος ("strength and courage")** — Od. 1.321 (Athena instills this in Telemachus upon departing) reuses, verbatim, the formula Athena applies to Diomedes before his aristeia in Il. V.2 — the same divine energizing-formula deployed at a critical threshold moment for a young/untested hero in both poems.
- **Διὸς βουλή / divine responsibility for events** — Od. 1.346–349 (Telemachus: singers aren't αἴτιοι, Zeus is αἴτιος) directly parallels the Moira–Zeus tension line logged for Il. XVI (Sarpedon episode) — the same principle of ultimate divine causation applied to a much smaller domain (a song's content) rather than battlefield fate.
